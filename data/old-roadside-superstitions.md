# Old Roadside Superstitions

## The Third Lantern

Travelers once believed that seeing three lanterns burning in an empty stretch of road meant an old settlement was nearby, even when no ruins could be found. Some swore the lights belonged to people who had died before their village was built.

## Moss Bells

Certain pale mosses were said to tremble before heavy magic reached an area. Children were taught to touch the moss and listen for its tiny ringing sound before entering old forests.

## The Sleeping Nail

Dwarven travelers sometimes carried a single iron nail with no practical purpose. If it became warm while resting on a table, tradition said someone in the household was keeping a secret.

## Nine-Luck Cats

Feline-Kin sailors believed a cat appearing on the prow before sunrise was a sign that the voyage would survive the worst weather. Seeing the same cat twice was considered either extraordinarily fortunate or deeply suspicious.

## Dragon Teeth in Rivers

River communities once claimed that white stones shaped like teeth were shed by dragons traveling upstream. Geologists later identified many as ordinary mineral formations, though fishermen still refuse to throw them back once found.

## The Quiet Star

Some old shepherds believed a star that disappeared for a single night was watching a particular family. The family was expected to leave a bowl of clean water outside until the star returned.

## Candle-Salt

In several frontier villages, salt was sprinkled around a candle during storms. If the flame leaned toward the salt, people believed something unseen had crossed the threshold.

## The Blue Door

An old elven superstition warned travelers never to knock on a blue door in a forest unless they knew the name of the person living behind it. The belief survived long after most people forgot why.

## Forge Dreams

Dwarven apprentices were once told that dreaming of a cold forge meant they had forgotten an important promise. Dreaming of a forge burning white meant the opposite: a promise had remembered them.

## The Spark's First Fire

An old human saying claims that every settlement has one fire that existed before its first house. The saying is not meant literally. It refers to the idea that every community begins with somebody willing to keep hope alive before there is anything to protect.

## River Coins

Travelers sometimes leave a coin beneath a bridge before crossing. The practice began as payment to ferrymen but eventually became a charm for safe passage.

## Ash on the Window

Some households mark a tiny line of ash beside a window before sleeping. It is supposed to show whether anything entered during the night. Most families know it cannot truly prove anything, but the tradition persists.

## The Empty Chair

In old mourning customs, one chair was left empty during the first feast after a death. Nobody was permitted to sit there until the family considered the mourning period complete.

## The Red Moon's Shadow

Children in several regions are warned that standing directly beneath the shadow of a red moon will make their dreams strange. Adults generally dismiss this, although some still close their shutters during lunar eclipses.

## The Last Bread

A traveling custom says the last piece of bread should never be eaten alone. Even enemies were traditionally offered a share if they happened to reach a camp when only one piece remained.

## Old Names

Some of these beliefs are thousands of years old and survive in fragments. Their original meanings are often lost, leaving modern peoples with rituals whose beginnings belong to civilizations that no longer exist.

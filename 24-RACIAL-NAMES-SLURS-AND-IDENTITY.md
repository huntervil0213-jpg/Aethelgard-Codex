# 24 — PEOPLE, NAMES, SLURS & IDENTITY

## Why this exists

Aethelgard's peoples do not all call one another the same thing. Names can change with region, history, class, war, friendship, and prejudice. This file records respectful names, old names, insults, slurs, and the circumstances in which characters might actually use them.

A slur is not automatically a synonym for a race. The same word can be affectionate in one community, neutral in another, and hateful in a third. Context matters.

## THE SPARK — HUMANS

**Spark** is an old poetic name for humans. It does not mean humans were the first race.

Ancient traditions describe humans as the **Spark of Life** because humans possessed an unusual capacity to begin again: to learn from other peoples, adapt to unfamiliar lands, build new communities, and carry ideas between civilizations. The phrase eventually shortened to **Spark**.

Some elves use it affectionately. Some dwarves use it with teasing respect. Some ancient peoples use it critically, arguing that humans' ability to spread and adapt also enabled conquest.

Therefore:

- Spark = culturally significant term for human
- It is not a claim that humans were the first people
- It is not inherently an insult
- Context determines whether it is affectionate, neutral, sarcastic, or contemptuous

## ELVES

Common respectful names include **elf**, **elven**, and culture-specific names such as **High Elven** when referring to language or court tradition.

Possible hostile terminology can emerge from political conflicts, particularly toward Spire-associated elves or toward elves associated with specific houses. Such terms should be recorded with their historical origin rather than treated as universal elf vocabulary.

### Skull-Veil terminology

**Skull-Veil** is a nickname used for the feared elven Inquisitors and the culture/order associated with them in the novel's established context. It is not the name of every elf and must never be used for unrelated creatures.

The nickname carries fear and hatred because of what these Inquisitors have done. Citizens may lower their voices, children may be warned away from them, and enemies may use harsher language when discussing them.

## DWARVES

Respectful: dwarf, dwarven.

Friendly cultural terms can refer to craft, clan, forge, stone, or beard traditions. Hostile terminology may attack a dwarf's clan, craft quality, greed stereotype, or perceived stubbornness.

Thrum's use of dwarven curses should feel culturally different from human profanity.

## FELINE-KIN

Respectful: Feline-Kin, feline-kin, or the person's own community name.

Insults may target their ears, tail, claws, hunting instincts, perceived curiosity, or stereotypes about stealth. Such language should vary by speaker and social environment.

Kiki may reclaim teasing terminology among friends that would be offensive from a stranger.

## DRAGONBORN

Respectful: Dragonborn.

Hostile language can target draconic ancestry, perceived pride, elemental blood, scales, or comparisons to lesser draconic creatures.

Calling a Dragonborn merely a "lesser dragon" is deliberately insulting in cultures that regard Dragonborn as an independent people.

## DRAGONS

Dragons have their own distinctions between species, lineages, ages, and elemental traditions. Calling an ancient dragon a beast is not merely inaccurate; it can be a deliberate humiliation.

## ORCS

The word **orc** is neutral when used respectfully. Imperial records have historically attached violent stereotypes to it. Some communities therefore distinguish their own clan or nation name from the broad category used by outsiders.

## GOBLINS, HOBGOBLINS & BUGBEARS

These are related peoples but are not interchangeable terms. Calling one people another can be neutral ignorance, teasing, or deliberate insult depending on context.

## TIEFLINGS

Respectful: Tiefling.

Hostile terminology frequently targets horns, tails, infernal ancestry, fiendish stereotypes, or assumptions about morality.

## AASIMAR

Respectful: Aasimar.

Hostile terminology can target divine ancestry, perceived superiority, or assumptions that every Aasimar serves the same celestial power.

## GENASI

Respectful: Genasi, with elemental lineage specified when relevant.

Insults can target elemental appearance or imply that a Genasi is merely an unstable elemental rather than a person.

## GNOMES

Respectful: gnome, gnomish.

Stereotypes often target size, eccentricity, invention, curiosity, or supposed inability to take danger seriously.

## HALFLINGS

Respectful: halfling.

Stereotypes can target size, rural origins, luck, food, or assumptions that halflings are harmless.

## GIANTS

Giants distinguish many peoples and lineages. Calling every giant a brute ignores enormous cultural differences between storm, frost, fire, stone, cloud, and other traditions.

## TABAXI & FELINE PEOPLES

Outsiders sometimes confuse Tabaxi with other feline peoples. This can be corrected through regional naming and cultural identity rather than assuming all feline races share one history.

## LIZARDFOLK

Lizardfolk should not be automatically equated with Dragonborn, Kobolds, or dragons. Their cultures and histories are distinct.

## KOBOLDS

Kobolds have a long and complicated relationship with draconic ancestry. Treating every kobold as a miniature Dragonborn is inaccurate and can be insulting.

## YUAN-TI

Yuan-ti identity is complicated by ancient magical transformation. Different societies have radically different attitudes toward them, ranging from fear to diplomacy to hatred.

## GITH

Gith communities have their own internal identities and historical divisions. Outsiders who collapse all Gith into one group may provoke immediate hostility.

## FEY

Fey terminology is especially dangerous because names, titles, and bargains can carry supernatural weight. A seemingly harmless insult may have cultural consequences beyond ordinary offense.

## RACIAL PREJUDICE IN THE NOVEL

Prejudice should come from characters, institutions, histories, and societies rather than from the narration declaring that a people is inherently bad.

When a hateful character uses a slur, the reader should understand something about the speaker, the conflict, or the culture behind it.

## KNOWN VS UNKNOWN TERMS

**Known in manuscript:** a term directly established by the novel or author.

**Historical expansion:** a term created for deeper Codex history and available for future use.

**Regional:** a term limited to a particular people or location.

**Unconfirmed:** an idea that should not be treated as canon until deliberately adopted.

## LANGUAGE RULE

Do not manufacture dozens of interchangeable slurs just to fill a list. A believable society has a smaller number of culturally meaningful insults that develop histories and change over time.
